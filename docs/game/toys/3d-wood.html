3D Wood - bonded fibre cells

What it is

The trunk is a complex of long fibre cells (growth ring × sector × length segment) with jittered corners, so no boundary is straight. Neighbouring cells are joined by bonds of three kinds and the material sets their strength: along (fibre continuity, across an end-grain face) is very strong, tang (between growth rings) is weak, and radial (across a ray plane, between neighbouring sectors) is weakest.

An axe strike snaps the struck cells' faces onto its cut plane and severs along-bonds in a thin band. A V of a slanted cut and a flat cut releases a chip, which tears free along the grain. A notch plus a back cut leave a hinge whose fibres tear at staggered heights. A wedge drives a crack along the cheapest path of ray bonds, layer by layer down the log.

Live stats

Good at

  • The notch, chips, hinge splinters and the split all come from the same bonds and differ only in which bonds break.
  • Cut faces lie exactly on the axe plane (sharp, flat), while split faces follow the jittered ray planes (fibrous). Nothing is a voxel step or a sphere.
  • Swapping the material record (oak vs pine, green vs seasoned) changes splinters, crack cost and checks without any per-material code.

Bad at

  • Cut planes snap whole cell faces, so a cut lands to within half a segment (5 cm) of where you aim. Finer cuts need more segments.
  • The falling trunk is one rigid body hinged at the notch apex, and the hinge only damps it. There is no stress solve, and no collision between the trunk and the stump.
  • Chips and split halves are kinematic: they fly and stop, but they don't tumble or collide.

In 3D / for our game:

This is the same bonded-cell machinery as rock; only the cell shape (long fibre prisms instead of irregular grains) and the bond law (anisotropic: along ≫ tang > radial) change. That alone makes chopping, felling and splitting look and behave like wood. Cells carry their own corners, so a cut can lie on any plane, and pieces are just body ids with a transform.

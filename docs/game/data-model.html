2. What the data must answer

The model should be picked by what gets asked of it, not by what it looks like. These are the questions, ordered by how often they're asked:

  1. What is the surface here? Drawing and collision, every frame, for everything in view.
  2. What material is at point p, and what's its state? Every tool hit or impact.
  3. If I hit here with this energy, where does it break? Every tool hit or impact.
  4. Which matter is still connected? After any break: what falls, and what becomes a separate piece.
  5. What changed? Saving, rewind, replay.

Question 3 is the one that separates this game from others. Most games answer it from the impact alone, for example a Voronoi pattern centred on the hit. We have to answer it from the material.

3. Defined, computed, stored: why untouched rock costs nothing

The owner asked: why would untouched rock ever need crack data, and why isn't it produced on demand? It is produced on demand. My earlier wording ("the rock comes from its crack pattern") made it sound stored. The model separates three states, and untouched rock is only in the first:

Why the rule has to exist before anyone touches the rock (instead of inventing cracks at the moment of the first hit):
  • Consistency. Two workers on opposite sides of the same outcrop must find the same crack. If cracks were invented per hit, the second worker's crack would depend on who hit first.
  • Rewind and replay. Re-running the same commands must give bit-identical rock. A pure function of position does this for free. Cracks that depend on hit order make replay fragile.
  • You can see cracks before touching. A cliff face shows its bedding and joints from a distance, and Roman quarrymen chose where to work by reading them. Rendering the untouched cliff needs the same rule, evaluated for drawing only.
  • Cost. A rule is O(1) memory however big the world is. Storing cracks per rock would grow with the world.
"Defined" is not "computed": nothing is evaluated for rock no one looks at or touches.
Open: whether the rule should be statistical crack families (section 4), or something simpler such as only bedding planes plus impact-driven cracks. That changes the rule, not the defined/computed/stored split.

6. Proposal: matter on a grid, cracks as planes (option C)

Neither pure option fits the game. B is what the geology looks like: cracks are planes, and blocks between them are convex. A is what work looks like: matter removed in any shape, damage anywhere, material varying point to point. C keeps both, so each answers the question it's good at:

Why C over B (convex cells only), which I proposed before: B can't do blueprint carving, can't vary material inside a block, and turns "dynamite to smithereens" into a cascade of fragile splits. Those are owner rulings, not edge cases.
Why C over A (voxels only): A loses the true crack angles and thin beds, and it throws away the free convex hulls the geology hands us. At 10 cm the staircase is about 1 px at the closest zoom, so A is closer than it looks. The thin-feature problem (slate, wood) is the real reason.
Why 10 cm: it's about one pixel at the closest zoom, so voxel steps never show as geometry. 0.2 m pieces (the grain cut-off) are 2 voxels across, so the smallest rigid pieces get their shape from planes and spike repair, not from the grid.
Open, and the deciding test: two cracks inside one 10 cm cell (thin slate beds, wood splinters). Options: a finer grid for that material, or several planes per cell. A test region of slate would decide it.

7. The 30° rule as physics, not geometry

The owner's point applies to every representation: the rule is enforced on the shape we output, by a pass that recognises spikes and repairs them. Thinking past "smoothing", there's a better reason for the repair than looks: a thin wedge of rock is physically weak. Real stone doesn't keep a needle tip because it chips off under the smallest load. So the rule can live in the material:

  • Matter level: every material has a minimum thickness. Voxels attached through less than that, such as a spike one voxel thick, break off as grains or dust. That conserves mass, and it's exactly what real rock does.
  • Mesh level: after meshing, any vertex with a face angle under 30° is cut off by a small plane. One tip becomes several corners, which is the owner's "jagged tip with several points". The same pass runs on wood chips.
  • Exceptions: carve mode and sharp weapons set a flag that skips both passes for that surface.

8. Data layout (data-oriented)

Laid out as flat arrays, grouped by what gets processed together. No objects, no pointers between them, only indices. Every array is either part of the rule (tiny), a cache (rebuildable), or the edit store (the save).

Why bricks of 8³ instead of one big grid per region: a quarried block is only a few bricks. Promoting it to a moving piece then means changing brick_owner, not copying data, and empty space costs nothing.
Why store commands, not results: the owner wants rewind and slow motion, never speed-up, on one machine. Determinism on one binary is cheap (fixed tick, fixed order, no fast-math), and then commands are by far the smallest save.

9. Promotion and demotion

Why return paths never lose anything: demotion only throws away the computed layer. Everything that changes future behaviour (removed matter, damage, a piece's new place) is in the stored layer before the cache is dropped. Recomputing gives the same region. That's the reversibility the owner asked about, and it falls out of the three-state split instead of needing a special "bake back" algorithm.

11. Weight: why the contact law, not mass, makes things feel heavy

In a standard game contact model, mass cancels out. A 10 t block and a 1 kg block of the same shape follow the same path, which is why games feel like empty boxes. Engineering rockfall software (RAMMS, research R1) does it differently:

  • No rebound off the ground (all downward speed is absorbed). Any hop comes from the shape: a corner hits and the piece pivots over an edge. A cube keeps about 6% of its energy per edge it rolls over, so it stops unless the slope is steep. That's the owner's "rolls only on very steep ground".
  • Friction grows as the piece gouges into the ground. Drag in contact scales with mass. The ground category (meadow, scree, bedrock) is data.
  • Pressure dents the ground above a threshold. A 2 m block presses about 50 kPa on the ground, the same order as soil strength, so heavy things visibly sink and light things don't. This is where mass stops cancelling out.
  • True scale, true gravity, small time step (1–2 ms). Big things then look slow, as in real video.
Unverified: nobody has compared this to real video yet, and the parameters come from 5–30 m/s rockfall, not 1–3 m/s drops. It needs a test against footage.
